Better antivenoms

Treat or Prevent Illness big idea

Venom-gland organoids that replace milking live snakes, and de novo designed proteins against conserved toxin families — a stable, broad, animal-free alternative to horse serum.

What it is

Snakebite kills an estimated 81,000–138,000 people per year and causes three times as many permanent disabilities — mostly in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa — yet the underlying antivenom technology has barely changed since the 1890s: horses are injected with venom, and their antibody-rich serum is purified and shipped. The resulting products are expensive, batch-variable, cold-chain dependent, and frequently trigger severe allergic reactions. A new generation of antivenoms aims to replace horse serum with precisely engineered proteins: recombinant monoclonal antibodies, nanobodies, and computationally designed miniproteins that target conserved toxin families shared across dozens of species.

Who is working on this

Hans Clevers at the Hubrecht Institute pioneered venom-gland organoids — miniature snake glands grown from stem cells that produce authentic venom in a lab dish, removing the need to farm and milk live snakes. David Baker’s lab at the University of Washington used deep learning to design miniproteins from scratch that bind and neutralize three-finger toxins, the dominant neurotoxin family in elapid venoms (cobras, mambas, kraits). A separate group, led by Timothy Patrick Jenkins in collaboration with Baker’s lab, published a nanobody-based recombinant antivenom in Nature (2025) that uses just eight antibody fragments targeting seven toxin subfamilies to achieve broad protection across African elapid species.

Why they are working on it

Snakebite is a WHO-designated neglected tropical disease, and most victims are rural poor in low-income countries where cold-chain antivenoms are unaffordable or physically unavailable. Current horse-serum antivenoms also carry meaningful risks of anaphylaxis and serum sickness, and their species-specificity means a single vial often fails against geographically adjacent snakes. Broadly neutralizing, heat-stable, recombinant alternatives could be manufactured consistently at industrial scale and distributed without refrigeration.

How they are working on it

Clevers’ group removed stem cells from the venom glands of multiple snake species, cultured them in organoid medium with growth factors, and showed that the resulting 3D structures secrete biologically active venom — enabling a living biobank of venom sources without maintaining dangerous snake colonies. Baker’s lab applied RFdiffusion and related deep learning tools to design miniproteins that physically grip conserved epitopes on short-chain and long-chain α-neurotoxins and cytotoxins; in mouse challenge experiments these proteins provided 80–100% survival against otherwise lethal doses. The nanobody approach screens large libraries of camelid heavy-chain antibody fragments against purified toxins, then combines the top hits into cocktails that cover the toxin diversity of an entire continent with a small, manufacturable set of reagents.
