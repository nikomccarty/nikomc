PCR is a (Surprisingly) Near-Optimal Technology

April 22, 2026 · 8 min read essay

The polymerase chain reaction turns 40 years old this year. In most fields, a technique that old would be a historical curiosity, long since replaced by something faster, cheaper, or more precise. But PCR is still everywhere — in hospitals, crime labs, field kits, and every molecular biology lab on earth. Why hasn't it been superseded?

The short answer is that PCR is close to the theoretical optimum for what it does. It amplifies a specific DNA sequence from a complex mixture with exceptional sensitivity and specificity.Sensitivity means it can detect a single molecule in a sample. Specificity means it amplifies only the target sequence and not off-target ones — assuming primer design is good. The basic thermodynamic constraints — DNA melting temperatures, primer annealing kinetics, polymerase extension rates — don't change regardless of what technology you use. PCR found a near-optimal path through that constraint landscape in 1985, and there isn't much room to improve.

How PCR works

Each PCR cycle has three steps: denaturation (heating to ~95°C to separate the two DNA strands), annealing (cooling to ~55–65°C so short primer sequences bind to the separated strands), and extension (72°C so a polymerase enzyme copies each strand from the primer).The exact temperatures depend on the GC content and length of your primers. Higher GC content raises the melting temperature. After 30 cycles, you have roughly 230 — about one billion — copies of the target sequence.

The key insight of Kary Mullis, who won the Nobel Prize for inventing PCR, was to use a heat-stable polymerase isolated from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium that lives in hot springs. Earlier attempts at the technique failed because the polymerase was destroyed by the high temperatures needed for denaturation.

Notice that even a small drop in efficiency has an outsized effect at high cycle counts. A reaction running at 95% efficiency for 30 cycles produces about half as many copies as one running at 100%. This is why reaction optimization matters so much in practice.In quantitative PCR (qPCR), efficiency is calibrated using a standard curve so the readout can be converted into absolute molecule counts.

The thermal limit

One real limit on PCR speed is the thermal cycling itself. You're heating and cooling a liquid, and that takes time. Modern thermocyclers have thin-walled tubes and powerful Peltier elements to speed up ramping, but there's a floor set by the heat capacity of water and the kinetics of DNA melting.

The fastest PCR systems today complete 30 cycles in about two minutes, down from the 3–4 hours Mullis's original protocol required. Most of that improvement came from hardware — thinner tube walls, faster ramp rates, smaller volumes — not from changes to the underlying chemistry.

Why it hasn't been replaced

Competing technologies exist: isothermal amplification (LAMP, RPA) doesn't require thermal cycling, which makes it attractive for point-of-care diagnostics.LAMP was used widely during COVID-19 for low-cost field testing. It's fast but harder to multiplex than PCR. But these methods have narrower dynamic ranges, higher false-positive rates, and are harder to multiplex. They solve specific problems in specific contexts. PCR remains the gold standard for most quantitative and high-specificity applications.

The lesson is that near-optimal technologies are stable in a way that merely good technologies aren't. When a technique has fully explored its constraint landscape, incremental improvements yield diminishing returns, and radical alternatives can't improve on the fundamentals. PCR is a near-optimal solution to a well-defined problem, which is why it looks, 40 years later, almost exactly the same as it did in 1985.

Further reading: Mullis et al., "Specific enzymatic amplification of DNA in vitro," Cold Spring Harbor Symposia on Quantitative Biology, 1986. Saiki et al., "Primer-directed enzymatic amplification of DNA with a thermostable DNA polymerase," Science, 1988.
