Hyperspectral Biology Fund

May 23, 2026 · 10 min read essay

Today I’m launching a $75,000 microgrant program to grow the field of hyperspectral biology.

At its core, hyperspectral biology aims to study life through the full spectrum of light it reflects or emits. It includes engineering organisms that emit molecules which absorb light in particular ways, such that we can “read out” their molecular states from far away, such as from satellites orbiting Earth.

Whereas a normal camera “collapses” light into just three channels (red, green, and blue; mimicking the human eye), hyperspectral cameras capture the full spectrum of wavelengths for every pixel in an image. They enable us to see parts of biology that are normally invisible to the naked eye. Unfortunately, these cameras also cost $10,000 to $100,000+.

NASA scientists built the first hyperspectral cameras in the early 1980s to map mineral deposits and algal blooms. Each type of molecule absorbs and reflects light in a distinct way, and so the idea was to mount these cameras on satellites and record the full spectrum for every pixel on the ground. Hyperspectral cameras are already used to quantify shifts in chlorophyll in plants, for example, which strongly absorb blue and red light, and to monitor moisture levels in soil.

It wasn’t until last year, though, that MIT scientists (led by Yonatan Chemla, a friend of mine) showed that you could also engineer living bacteria to emit molecules with unique “hyperspectral fingerprints” and then image those microbes from outer space.

For a study in Nature Biotechnology, Chemla and colleagues engineered two strains of bacteria to overproduce pigments (called biliverdin IXα and bacteriochlorophyll a) that absorb light in a distinctive way, meaning they each have a unique hyperspectral fingerprint. The researchers sprayed these engineered cells onto patches of soil at Fort Devens, a military base in Massachusetts, and then flew a hyperspectral drone overhead. By using a computer algorithm to separate the pigment’s signal from the background noise of dirt and sand, they could detect the locations of these microbes from up to 90 meters away. (In unpublished work, they did the same with a satellite orbiting hundreds of miles above Earth.)

But this is just the beginning of what’s possible!

Therefore, I’m giving out $75,000 in microgrants — ranging from $5,000 to $12,500 each — to help grow the field of hyperspectral biology. These grants are supported by the Experiment Foundation, and you can click here to apply for funding by July 10. I’ll entertain a huge range of ideas, but here are some things that I think would be particularly useful…

First, we need cheaper and (ideally) open-source hyperspectral cameras. This is a major bottleneck on people’s ability to enter this field. Most hyperspectral cameras on the market cost tens of thousands of dollars depending on how complicated they are, and most academic labs are not going to buy that to pursue risky endeavors. Most labs also don’t have the military connections that would let them access satellites with hyperspectral cameras. So it would be great if somebody could design open-source blueprints to build hyperspectral cameras. That would grow the field, because all of a sudden people could connect these cameras to microscopes, collect data across more wavelengths, and do all kinds of interesting things.

I have no idea how much it should cost to build a hyperspectral camera, but my prior investigations of things like PCR machines suggest you can usually build a technology for at least 10 times less than what it sells for on the market. So I strongly suspect it should be possible to buy the equipment to build one for about $1,000 or less. So if we can grow the field quickly, we can start doing interesting things with existing space infrastructure. The key bottleneck is the cost of these cameras on Earth and the biology itself. If every lab could afford one, or build one from open-source plans, then we could easily mount them on satellites to monitor crops and much more.

Second, we need foundational datasets of hyperspectral profiles! We literally need to point hyperspectral cameras at different molecules and varied types of cells, collect their spectrograms, and then release those data publicly.

When Chemla started his experiments, he scoured through chemical databases to find hyperspectral data on molecules. Surely people had already pointed hyperspectral cameras at purified molecules to see which wavelengths they absorb most strongly. But no! Only about 40 molecules have any hyperspectral data at all. Chemla spent months running computational simulations to find other molecules that might have unique hyperspectral fingerprints.

The dream would be that every biomolecule found in nature, every molecule that cells naturally synthesize, gets studied with a hyperspectral camera. In other words, we should get a full spectral profile for every biomolecule known to mankind. Some of these molecules will have really unique fingerprints that increase the resolution of this technology and let us see things more accurately. But basically nobody has ever done this, and I suspect it ties back to the first point, the need for cheaper open-source cameras.

This would also enable multiplexed hyperspectral imaging in the future. Say we want to engineer plants that detect 10 different pathogens. If a plant detects pathogen A, it emits hyperspectral molecule A, which has a unique fingerprint, and the same for B, and so on. You could then deconvolute many different hyperspectral signatures to record even more information in a specific spatial region on Earth. That would be very cool.

Third, we should “port” hyperspectral reporter genes into plants and other organisms. I would love for someone to engineer a plant, maybe a tobacco plant or something simple, then fly a drone with a hyperspectral camera overhead and see if you can detect the signature from the plants. Chemla has shown this works in bacteria, but there are reasons why bacteria might not be the best platform long-term. The regulations around releasing engineered microbes into the wild are extremely severe in basically every country on Earth, and it’s almost impossible to get approval unless it’s for an agricultural application or a food product like a probiotic.

Everything else goes through something called the Toxic Substances Control Act, which basically makes it impossible to do this outside of containment. I have an article coming up about this in Works in Progress magazine. It might be much easier to grow the field, and actually start using these applications outside the lab, if we could port these hyperspectral reporters into organisms with more well-tested regulatory pathways. Plants are the obvious example. We could imagine planting a few trees within a forest, each clearly marked, that are designed to sense a pathogen and then be detectable from satellites in space.

There’s also a lot of room to improve algorithms. We need better image detection algorithms, open-source computational tools that take hyperspectral images and deconvolute them for different biomolecules. I would love to see a web app where people could upload a hyperspectral image, say what their target molecule is, and have it automatically run these algorithms to see if it can pick out that signal. Existing approaches are basically academic test cases and not scalable, so there’s a lot to explore purely on the computational side.

That also goes for predicting the hyperspectral profiles of molecules. When Chemla was doing his work, the molecular databases had almost no data on hyperspectral profiles, so he had to run computational simulations to predict which molecules would have unique fingerprints. He did that entirely computationally. I suspect there’s an opportunity to use AI and other tools to find more molecules that would make good hyperspectral reporters.

I’m convinced that the main bottleneck in hyperspectral biology is the actual biology — the engineering of organisms and the foundational data sets needed to understand what we’re seeing. The rest of the infrastructure is already there. There are many companies launching hyperspectral satellites into space. There is Pixxel, of course, which is building what it calls the world’s highest-resolution hyperspectral satellite constellation, and these satellites are already orbiting Earth. There are companies like Orbital Sidekick and Wyvern, a Y Combinator-funded company, plus government satellites and companies like Planet.

These companies exist because you can do very useful things with hyperspectral cameras. You can monitor crop health and soil moisture. You can monitor methane emissions and oil spills. And you can find minerals and other resources, so some of these satellites are literally used to hunt for lithium and copper. That part of the ecosystem is up there. There are probably around 60 satellites with hyperspectral cameras already in orbit around Earth.

The grand vision is to have hyperspectral reporters everywhere, monitoring the things we care about. Engineer organisms to sense explosives, and you could map unexploded ordnance for cleanup. In Laos alone, the U.S. dropped more than 270 million cluster bomblets during the Vietnam War, and roughly 80 million failed to detonate; more than 20,000 people have died from them since the war ended, and about a quarter of the country’s villages remain contaminated. Engineer organisms to sense pathogens, and farms could flag viral outbreaks before any symptoms appear. That’s the beauty of biology: it can sense almost anything — ions, metals, toxins, pollutants, minerals — and we can wire that sensing to a hyperspectral output we read from space.

So the dream is a planetary-scale, autonomous biosensing network: living sensors blanketing Earth’s surface, their signals read automatically from orbit. We could catch outbreaks before they spread, spot oil spills and algae blooms, track biomass as it shifts with the seasons, watch forests change, and monitor the color of rivers. There is just so much you could do.

My plan is to give out 8 to 12 grants. Every recipient must release their data publicly, and I’ll check in every couple of months to see how things are going, though it’s fine if experiments fail, since some of these proposals should be risky. The funds can support experiments only, not events or workshops. I’m making these decisions alongside Yonatan Chemla and Andrew York, a physicist who builds microscopes at the Chan Zuckerberg Biohub.

Of course, $75,000 won’t grow this field as much as it deserves. It’s a starting point, and one small piece of a domain that has raised maybe $10 million total so far. Plenty of others are already working here. DARPA has been a major funder; it backed the MIT work Chemla was part of, and has funded groups at Purdue studying whether plants show observable spectral responses to synthetic chemicals (LeafSpec LLC now holds some of the resulting patents). So people are already pushing on both fronts: engineering organisms to produce hyperspectral signatures, and probing natural organisms for signals we can already read. That second path would ease the regulatory pipeline considerably.

My main goal right now is to spread awareness and fund small experiments that de-risk the field’s key bottlenecks. If these grants work out, I hope larger philanthropists will follow.
