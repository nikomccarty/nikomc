Biology doesn't have the culture of rapid experimentation and community-driven prototyping that computing does. Microgrants are one way to incentivize more of these acts. Below are some people who I've supported with small amounts of funding. This money, historically, has come from Astera Institute, Experiment Foundation, and ResearchHub. I typically advertise new funding opportunities on my social media pages.

Recipients

Haizhao Yang & Youran Sun $5,000

Accelerating Hyperspectral Biology with AI Agents. To build domain-specialized AI agents that can carry a hyperspectral analysis end to end: defining the task, proposing several algorithms, writing and running the code, comparing results, and then auditing the evidence for errors such as data leakage or failure on new conditions. They will evaluate the agents on public hyperspectral reporter data across varying backgrounds, light levels, and imaging distances.

Bryan Duoto $6,000

Hyperspectral Characterization of Carotenoid- and Retinal-Producing Halophiles. To measure carotenoid- and retinal-producing halophiles with hyperspectral cameras. Salt-loving microbes produce pigments such as bacterioruberin and bacteriorhodopsin, which may act as optical fingerprints detectable from the air. He will fly a calibrated drone-mounted VNIR system over 30 sites in the Great Salt Lake's North Arm, pairing the imagery with shotgun metagenomics and HPLC pigment measurements. The lake has lost roughly two-thirds of its surface area since 1850.

Joao Victor Dias $2,500

HyperMix: open-source detection of engineered biosignatures in remote hyperspectral imagery. Engineered bacteria have been spotted from 90 meters away, but the signal is faint and hides inside the colors of soil, leaves, and water. He will build a physics-based scene simulator with known ground truth, reproduce the classical baselines, and train a physics-informed network that detects and unmixes against unknown backgrounds. MIT-licensed, with a public benchmark.

Marisa Merino $5,500

A foundational open reference dataset of epithelial tissue states using label-free hyperspectral imaging. No shared open reference currently links spectral signatures to independently verified states in living tissue. Working in the Drosophila wing epithelium, she will image healthy, wounded, and apoptotic tissue under matched ex vivo conditions, then use fluorescent markers to confirm each state so that every spectrum is anchored in known biology.

Nathan Haasbroek & Enrique Asin-Garcia $10,000

Multiplexed microbial whole-cell biosensing of environmental pollutants using hyperspectral reporters. Ecosystems are usually exposed to mixtures of contaminants rather than single compounds, but too few compatible reporters exist for multiplexed sensing. They will engineer predicted hyperspectral reporters in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Pseudomonas putida, validate their actual spectra, and integrate them pairwise to determine which reporter pairs can be told apart.

Sean Jungbluth $5,000

How can a cheap scanner reveal hidden signals in living soil? Before a biological signal can be separated from its background, someone has to measure the background well. He will build a benchtop scanner from an off-the-shelf mini-spectrometer and a light-controlled chamber, then test whether it yields repeatable soil reflectance curves across moisture, texture, and organic matter gradients. Spectra, code, bill of materials, and build guide all released.

Cassandra Collins $3,230

Spectral fingerprints of soil biology: hyperspectral imaging of fungi and plant roots in the soil environment. Fungal hyphae are only a few microns wide and can resemble roots or organic matter in visible-light images. Using reflected-light hyperspectral microscopy at UCSB, she will scan soil aggregates from coastal temperate rainforest sites to test whether hyphae, roots, minerals, and organic material are spectrally separable, and which wavelengths carry the most information.

Chaim Elchik $10,000

A Hyperspectral “Colour Cloud” Framework for Detecting Environmental Disruption of Avian Visual Signals. Millions of specimens already record environmental conditions, such as historical black carbon pollution, within their plumage. He will image them at pixel-level resolution and develop an unsupervised “colour cloud” clustering algorithm to localize anomalous colour variation without a priori bias. Natural history collections as a massive distributed sensor network, extending monitoring backward in time.

Maxwell Wilson & Maya Sampson $11,300

A Spectral Atlas of Nature’s Metabolites: Reading the light-absorbance spectra of life, one molecule at a time. Absorbance spectra are recorded constantly, since most LC runs pass eluent through a diode-array detector, and then discarded. The bottleneck is not instrumentation; nobody has run it systematically across chemical space. Using the UCSB foundry's copy of the NCI natural-products library, they will validate a pipeline on authentic standards and publish every structure-linked spectrum openly, with full acquisition metadata.
